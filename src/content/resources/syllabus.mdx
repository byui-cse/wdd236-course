## Description

WDD 236: Frontend Architecture and Engineering bridges the gap between raw coding and professional product engineering.

This course is competency based and student directed. Instead of completing a fixed sequence of assignments, you choose what to work on each week, write a learning contract, build and investigate, and then show your instructor or TA what you can do.

The competencies span planning frontend products (sitemaps, wireframes, and design tokens), building them from browser-native components and modular JavaScript, testing them automatically, managing asynchronous data and state, and making them accessible. An optional competency adds a team project focused on quality, performance, and deployment.

A key feature of this course is that you will use AI to generate most of your code. Your work is to specify what to build, inspect and test what the AI produces, find its mistakes, and explain the result. You remain accountable for correctness, clarity, and professional standards.

By the end of the course, students will have experience planning, building, testing, and shipping frontend products using professional engineering workflows.

## Prerequisites

**WDD 235 (Web Mechanics)**

This course assumes you have already developed foundational web skills. You should be able to:

1. Explain the semantic purpose of various HTML elements and their role in document structure.
2. Utilize CSS in a web layout.
3. Use JavaScript for basic programming and DOM manipulation.
4. Explain the interaction between a client and a server.
5. Explain the benefit of separating presentation, content, and logic.
6. Build and deploy a working application to the Internet.

If you feel rusty, review:

1. You should be very comfortable writing HTML and modern CSS. If you need a review you can read [MDN HTML Introduction](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn/Getting_started_with_the_web/HTML_basics) and [MDN CSS Introduction](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn/Getting_started_with_the_web/CSS_basics).
2. You should be comfortable with JavaScript fundamentals including variables, data types, functions, loops, and conditionals. If you need a review you can read [MDN JavaScript First Steps](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn/Getting_started_with_the_web/JavaScript_basics).
3. You should be comfortable creating websites with template-based tools using a component-based architecture.
4. Make sure you are comfortable with basic DOM manipulation with JavaScript. If you need a review you can read [MDN Manipulating Documents](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn/JavaScript/Client-side_web_APIs/Manipulating_documents).
5. Make sure you are comfortable with basic event management with JavaScript. If you need a review you can read [MDN Introduction to Events](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn/JavaScript/Building_blocks/Events).
6. You should be comfortable using Git and GitHub for collaborative workflows.

## Outcomes

By taking advantage of the opportunities this course presents, you will develop fundamental knowledge and skills in computing and technology and improve your professional possibilities by:

1. Translate user needs into technical blueprints using sitemaps, wireframes, and design system documentation.
2. Apply automated testing (unit and integration) to ensure application logic is bug-resistant.
3. Architect and implement advanced JavaScript patterns, including asynchronous flow and modular architecture.
4. Implement A11y patterns to ensure products meet professional WCAG standards.
5. Evaluate and refine AI-generated code, test suites, and code reviews to ensure technical accuracy.

These outcomes extend WDD 235 outcomes from foundational mechanics into professional frontend engineering practice.

## Course Structure

### How the course flows

There is no fixed weekly lecture or assignment list. Each week follows the same cycle:

1. **Choose a focus.** Pick a competency and the specific slices (small, assessable pieces of a competency) you will work on. The [Competency Guide](../competencies/) lists them all. If you are unsure what to pick, follow the [recommended 10-contract path](../../ponder/recommended-10-contract-path/).
2. **Write a learning contract.** Describe your starting point, a guiding question, your plan, the challenges you will test, and the evidence you will produce. See [Create a Learning Contract](../../ponder/learning-contract/).
3. **Build with AI and investigate.** Specify the work, have AI generate it, then inspect, test, and revise it. See the [AI-assisted development workflow](../../ponder/ai-workflow/).
4. **Review with a peer.** Demonstrate your work, try to break each other's work, and record what you learned. See [Peer Contract Review](../../ponder/contract-review/).
5. **Pass off.** When the slices of a competency are reviewed, show your instructor or TA that you can perform, explain, and adapt the work, including a short live variation. See [Competency Pass-Off](../../ponder/competency-pass-off/).
6. **Choose your next contract.** Use the feedback to pick your next slices. Slices you have not yet passed off roll forward.

The first week is shared: everyone builds the same small component so that the class learns the process together (see the [model first learning contract](../../ponder/model-first-learning-contract/)). After that, your path is your own.

### The competencies

You will show what you can do in six required competencies. Each is divided into slices.

1. Frontend information architecture and architectural blueprinting
2. Component engineering and modular architecture
3. Automated test engineering
4. Asynchronous flow and state management
5. Inclusive craftsmanship and accessibility compliance
6. AI oversight, code auditing, and quality assurance

There is also one optional competency:

7. Performance and deployment, completed on a team project

Debugging and responsible AI use are part of every contract rather than separate topics.

You are expected to complete about one contract per week for roughly ten weeks, leaving the rest of the semester for pass-offs, retries, and the optional team project. Pass off competencies as you finish them rather than waiting until the end.

## Grading Policy

Treat this course as pass/fail with a grade tier. Your grade is based on the competencies you pass off, not on points or the number of assignments you complete.

### What it takes to pass off

A slice is passed off when you have:

- A working artifact.
- An explanation of your important decisions in your own words.
- A verification (test, audit, or experiment) that you ran yourself.
- A discussion of one problem, limitation, or tradeoff.

A competency is passed off when every slice has been reviewed with a peer and you demonstrate it to the instructor or TA, including a short live variation. You are rated at one of three levels:

- **Not yet:** The evidence is missing or you cannot explain or adapt the work. Choose a next step and try again.
- **Proficient:** You meet the evidence, explain your decisions, and handle the variation. This is required to pass.
- **Advanced:** Proficient, plus a harder case, a justified tradeoff, a non-obvious problem solved, or the ability to teach it.

You may re-attempt a pass-off after feedback. "Not yet" is feedback, not a failure.

### Your grade

- **B:** Pass off all six required competencies (every slice) at Proficient or better.
- **A:** Earn a B, and also either demonstrate Advanced mastery on four or more competencies, or pass off the optional Performance and Deployment competency at Proficient or better.
- **F:** If you do not pass off all six required competencies at Proficient or better, you will receive an F.

## AI Usage Policy

AI is expected in this course. You will use it to generate most of your code, following the specify, generate, inspect, test, revise, and explain workflow. AI should *not* replace your own engineering judgment. The specification, the review, the verification, and the decisions are yours.

In WDD 236, you should use AI to:

- Generate code from specifications that you wrote and understand.
- Challenge your architecture assumptions.
- Suggest edge cases your tests may be missing, then check them yourself.
- Flag potential accessibility or maintainability risks.

You must be able to explain, change, and defend everything you submit. You are responsible for validating all AI output. Keep your prompts, verification notes, and corrections, because they are evidence for the AI oversight competency.

Each contract states how you will use AI and how you will verify the result. Some activities have a stoplight indicator that gives more specific direction.
